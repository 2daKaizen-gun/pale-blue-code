## Scope

sub-11-3 measured how often the hunt cries wolf (false-alarm rate, zero across five nulls);
sub-11-4 measured how deep it can see (completeness floor, ~0.40%). Both describe the
**instrument**. sub-11-5 asks the question that has to come before any fetch: on these stars,
would a dip we could detect even be a planet? A transit hides a fraction `depth` of the star's
disk, and that disk is set by the star's radius, so

```
R_planet = sqrt(depth) * R_star
```

The same 0.40% dip is a small planet on a dwarf and a star-sized companion on a giant. The
frontier pool is bright *because* many of its stars are giants — so the detection floor, read
through each star's radius, becomes a **minimum detectable planet radius** that on most of the
pool is far too large to be a planet at all. This sub-phase computes that, and turns the flat
50-star pool into a ranked target list separating the few real targets from the many mirages.

## What a detection would mean (L1–L2)

The fiducial detection floor is **0.40%** (the sub-11-4 completeness floor), applied uniformly.
Read through each pool star's radius, over the whole 50-star pool:

```
POOL (50 stars)            plausible  too_big  unknown
                              16        34        0

STELLAR RADIUS (R_sun)     min 0.96   median 10.80   max 111.19
```

The pool is giant-dominated — a median radius of **10.8 R_sun**. The ranked target list, at the
ends and at the planet/star boundary (2.0 R_jup):

```
  rank  TIC          R_star      R_planet,min      class
  ----  -----------  ---------   -------------      ---------
   1    156004112    0.96 R_sun   0.60 R_jup (6.6 R_earth)  plausible   <- best target
   2    231631019    0.97 R_sun   0.61 R_jup               plausible
   5    219990936    1.69 R_sun   1.06 R_jup               plausible
  16    382252939    3.10 R_sun   1.95 R_jup               plausible   <- last plausible
  17    206541781    3.33 R_sun   2.09 R_jup               too_big     <- first star
  50    165880227  111.19 R_sun  69.98 R_jup               too_big     <- a whole star
```

Read it straight: **only 16 of 50** stars are small enough (R_star < ~3.2 R_sun) that our floor
implies a planet-sized companion. On the other 34 a detectable dip would be a star. And the
honest sting is at the top: even the *best* target, a 0.96 R_sun dwarf, only reaches down to
**0.60 R_jup = 6.6 R_earth** — a Neptune-to-Saturn, not an Earth. Our floor on this fresh bright
sky buys sub-Saturns around dwarfs, nothing smaller. The pool is not a field of Earths waiting;
it is a short list of a-few-Neptunes-if-we-are-lucky, and 34 places where a "detection" is a
mirage by construction.

### Why radius-first, not a magnitude-noise model

Stellar radius spans **~100x** across the pool (0.96 → 111 R_sun) while photometric noise spans
only ~10x, so which stars can host a *detectable planet* is set by radius, not by a fragile
per-star noise-from-magnitude fit. The three already-fetched pool giants measure 158 / 268 /
1245 ppm per point — far above the photon limit — so a giant is noisier **and** bigger, and using
one fiducial floor understates their true noise. That makes every `too_big` verdict conservative
(their real floor is deeper, their real minimum planet even bigger), never optimistic. The
verdict we most want to trust — "this is too big to be a planet" — is the one the method is
safest on.

---

## Size, not mass (the honest boundary)

A transit depth fixes a companion's **radius**, not its mass. Gas giants and brown dwarfs both
sit near 1 R_jup (electron degeneracy holds the radius roughly flat across a wide mass range),
and an inflated hot giant can reach ~2 R_jup; above that a transiting body is almost certainly
stellar. So `plausible` means *planet-sized*, not *a planet* — a real candidate would still need
a mass (radial velocity, or a dynamical constraint). This is triage that says where a detection
*could* be a world and where it *cannot*; it is not itself a detection. What it buys is a place
to point: fetch the 16, not the 50, and never mistake a giant's dip for a planet.

## Honest boundary (carried forward)

The verdict is a **size** gate, not a planethood claim, and it rests on one fiducial floor
(0.40%) rather than a per-star noise model — a choice justified by radius dominating the pool's
100x spread, and conservative for the giants. The floor itself is a *detection* floor, not a
completeness guarantee (sub-11-4). And the classification uses the catalog stellar radius; a
wrong radius moves a star across the boundary — the enriched pool's radii are TIC-catalog values,
good enough to triage but not to certify. What sub-11-5 gives is the thing that had to come
before the fetch: a ranked short list of 16 stars where a single-transit detection could be a
world, and 34 where it could only be a star. The next question is no longer *which sky* but
*whether the hunt itself honors this triage* — the pool hunt still searches all 50 and ranks its
survivors by significance alone, blind to the fact that a giant's dip cannot be a planet. Making
the hunt carry each star's size verdict is sub-11-6.

## Result

The pool is no longer a flat list of 50 fresh stars. It is a ranked target list that says, per
star, the smallest planet a detection there could reveal — and names honestly that only 16 are

places where a dip would be a star. Paired with the two scoreboards, the hunt now knows not only
its reach and its false-alarm rate, but where in the sky that reach is worth spending.
